1. How we did the analysis

We tested how Reynolds & Co shows up when a buyer, rather than an existing customer, asks an AI assistant about placing a steel structures job.

  • Ten questions that follow a real buying path, from "we need a steel frame for a six-storey office, who can design and install it?" to "what does Reynolds & Co do?"
  • Across the four main AI assistants buyers use: ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Perplexity.
  • We typed each question in by hand, the same way a real buyer would, rather than through an automated tool. That is the only way to see exactly what a buyer sees, and to compare the assistants fairly, like for like.
  • Every answer and the websites it relied on were recorded word for word. For your closest named competitor, Meridian Structures, we asked one direct question about it, and noted whether it came up in the buyer questions.

In plain terms: the ten buyer questions for Reynolds & Co (40 runs), plus one direct question about Meridian as a benchmark (4 runs), all live, across four AI assistants.

2. What we found

When buyers ask AI who should build their steel frame, Reynolds & Co does not come up. When they ask about it by name, AI describes the business accurately. So the problem is being found, not being believed. That is good news, because being found is faster and cheaper to fix than being believed.

The category is open

All four gave generic advice and moved on. No brand owns "a steel contractor who can work a tight city-centre site" in AI answers. That space is there to be taken.

Why these ten questions

We did not pick them at random. They follow the path a real buyer takes, from a first requirement to naming a firm, and each step is a chance to be found.

  • Questions 1 to 3. A buyer with a job to place who does not yet know which firms exist, including the phrase most of them actually type and the one that asks for a specialist rather than a big fabricator.
  • Questions 4 to 6. A buyer with a real constraint: a tight site, a budget, a sector with its own rules.
  • Questions 7 to 9. A buyer checking they are shortlisting the right kind of firm, on accreditation, on scope, and on where you are.
  • Question 10. A buyer who already knows the name.

If the firm is going to be found, it has to appear somewhere along that path. Today it only appears at the very end, once the buyer already knows to ask for it by name.

What the pattern means

The problem is being found, not being described

Reynolds & Co is absent from every requirement, category, constraint and cost question, and only appears in passing on three of them, always at the bottom of a list. All four assistants described the business accurately once it was named. The buyers most likely to want a specialist are sent straight to the large fabricators that Reynolds competes against.

Two listings are doing all the work

Across the 36 runs where the buyer did not type the name, Reynolds appeared six times, every one of them in passing. Trace the sources and they come back to two places: a trade directory entry and a regional builders' list. That is the entire visible footprint. It also proves the mechanism, because two citations were enough to put Reynolds in front of three different assistants. The fix is more of them, not a different strategy.

The one small opening was the regional question

The only place Reynolds surfaced on three assistants out of four was question 9, on specialist firms in Yorkshire, and it came from the regional listing rather than from anything on your own site. Local intent is already half working, which makes it the cheapest thing to push. Questions 4 and 5, the constrained site and the cost, returned no contractor names at all from any assistant, so those are uncontested and worth taking next.

Compared with Meridian

Both firms are described accurately when named, and the descriptions are fair. The difference is what stands behind the name. When an assistant digs into Meridian, it finds two trade titles, a directory profile, a Wikipedia entry and a set of named project case studies. For Reynolds & Co it finds your own website, one trade directory line and a regional listing. On Perplexity, which shows its sources, the Meridian answer carried four citations and the Reynolds answer carried one.

The gap is independent coverage, not the quality of the work, where the two are level. Every publication in Meridian's stack is open to Reynolds, which is what makes this fixable inside a quarter.
