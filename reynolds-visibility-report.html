Summary

Three short parts: how we did the analysis, what we found, what to do about it. Read only this and you'll know what matters.

1. How we did the analysis

We tested how Reynolds & Co shows up when a buyer, not an existing customer, asks an AI assistant about a steel structures job.

  • Ten questions that follow a real buying path, from "we need a steel frame for a six-storey office, who can do it?" to "what does Reynolds & Co do?"
  • Across the four assistants buyers actually use: ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Perplexity.
  • For Meridian Structures, your named competitor, we asked one direct question as a benchmark, to see what sits behind the name when an assistant already has it.
  • We typed every question in by hand rather than through an automated tool. That's the only way to see what a buyer sees, including which websites the answer leaned on.
  • Every answer and every source it cited was recorded word for word.

In plain terms: 44 live runs, 40 for Reynolds and 4 for the Meridian benchmark, on one afternoon in August 2026.

2. What we found

When buyers ask AI who should build their steel frame, Reynolds doesn't come up. When they ask about Reynolds by name, AI describes the business accurately. So the problem is being found, not being explained.

The Test: what AI does today

This is the scoreboard we measure now, and again at the end, to prove the change. It's exactly what the four assistants said when we asked the ten buyer questions.

Why these ten questions

We didn't pick them at random. They follow the path a real buyer takes, from a first requirement to naming a firm, and every step along it is a chance to be found.

  • Questions 1 to 3. A buyer with a job to place who doesn't yet know which firms exist, including the phrase most of them actually type and the one that asks for a specialist rather than a big fabricator.
  • Questions 4 to 6. A buyer with a real constraint: a tight site, a budget, a sector with its own rules.
  • Questions 7 to 9. A buyer checking they're shortlisting the right kind of firm, on accreditation, on scope, and on where you are.
  • Question 10. A buyer who already knows the name.

To be found, Reynolds has to appear somewhere along that path. Today it appears at the very end, once the buyer already knows to ask.

What the pattern means

The problem is being found, not being described

All four assistants got Reynolds right on question 10. The description was accurate, current and fair. Worth knowing, because it means nothing needs correcting. The work is getting the name into the answer in the first place.

Two listings are doing all the work

Across the 36 runs where the buyer didn't type the name, Reynolds appeared six times, every one of them in passing. Trace the sources and they come back to two places: a trade directory entry and a regional builders' list. That's the entire visible footprint. It also proves the mechanism, because two citations were enough to put Reynolds in front of three different assistants. The fix is more of them, not a different strategy.

Where the openings are

Question 9, the regional one, is the only place Reynolds already shows up on three assistants out of four. Local intent is half-working and can be pushed. Questions 4 and 5, the constrained site and the cost, returned no contractor names at all from any assistant, so they're uncontested. Those three are the ones to go after first.

The Meridian benchmark

We asked each assistant one direct question about Meridian Structures, your closest named competitor. Not to score them against you, but to see what an assistant has behind a name it already knows.

Both firms are described accurately when named. The difference is the sources. When an assistant answered the Reynolds question, it cited reynoldsco.co.uk and nothing else. When it answered the Meridian question, it cited two trade titles, a directory and a Wikipedia entry, then named Meridian with visibly more confidence because more than one source agreed. On Perplexity, which shows its citations, the Meridian answer carried four; the Reynolds answer carried one.

That gap is buildable. Meridian's advantage is a stack of third-party citations rather than a better business, and every publication in that stack is open to Reynolds.
